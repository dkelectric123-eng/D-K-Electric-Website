Why Your Breaker Keeps Tripping

First: a tripping breaker is not broken

A breaker exists to cut power when a circuit draws more current than the wire behind it can safely carry. When it trips, it has just done the one job it has. The breaker is the messenger.

That is why resetting it over and over is the wrong instinct. Nothing has been fixed, the underlying fault is still there, and every reset puts heat into the panel. The useful question is not how to stop it tripping. It is what changed on that circuit.

The four things it usually is

Too much on one circuit

The most common cause and the least alarming. A space heater, a window air conditioner, a microwave and a kettle on the same run will trip a 15 amp breaker without anything being wrong. If it trips at a predictable moment — when the heater comes on, when you start the dryer — this is almost always it. The fix is a dedicated circuit, not a bigger breaker.

A short circuit

A hot wire touching a neutral or a ground. This trips instantly and hard, usually the moment something is plugged in or switched on. Sometimes there is a pop or a scorch mark. A short does not come and go — if it is a short, it is repeatable.

A ground fault

Current escaping to ground somewhere it should not. Common in kitchens, bathrooms, garages, basements and outdoors, which is exactly where GFCI outlets are required. If a GFCI keeps tripping, it is usually finding something real — moisture in an outdoor box is a classic.

The breaker itself is worn out

Breakers are mechanical and they age. One that has tripped hundreds of times over twenty years can start tripping at less than its rated load. This is real but it is the last thing to conclude, not the first — a breaker blamed for a fault it was correctly detecting is a dangerous mistake.

When to stop and call the same day

Most tripping is an inconvenience. These are not:

  • A burning smell near the panel, an outlet or a switch
  • Scorch marks or discolouration on a breaker, an outlet or a cover plate
  • A buzzing or crackling sound from the panel
  • A breaker that is hot to the touch
  • A breaker that will not reset at all, or trips again immediately

Any of those means something is overheating, and overheating is how electrical fires start. Switch that breaker off if you can do it safely, stop using the circuit, and call.

Why guessing gets expensive

From the outside every one of these looks the same: something stopped working. Swapping parts until the symptom goes away is how a small problem becomes a big bill. Tracing a fault properly means testing the circuit under load, checking terminations, and isolating the branch before anything gets replaced.
